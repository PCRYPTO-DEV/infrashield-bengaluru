# Pebble Cartography

*A design philosophy*

Pebble Cartography holds that a world can be crossed in a handful of steps, if each step is a stone big enough to stand on. Space is not filled; it is **paced**. A few large, perfectly round forms rest in wide calm fields, and a single unbroken thread runs between them, the way a trail of pebbles runs through a wood. Nothing floats alone: every form is tethered to the one before it and the one after it. The eye never asks *where next?* because the path has already answered. The composition is a walk, not a menu.

Form is reduced to the shapes a hand learns first: the circle, the hexagon, the line that bends without breaking. Circles carry meaning and hexagons carry ground. The hexagonal lattice lies underneath everything as a faint, patient texture, thousands of identical cells laid with the care of a mosaicist. It is barely visible, yet it makes the open space feel surveyed and trustworthy. Above it, the stones sit with deliberate weight, each one turned, sized and placed through countless small adjustments until the whole feels inevitable. This is the product of painstaking attention: no form is approximate, no curve is casual, and every tangent is resolved.

Colour is a language a child already speaks. Green, amber, orange and red are borrowed from the street, where they already mean *go*, *careful* and *stop*. A single clear blue means *you are here*, and one small shock of pink marks what someone wished you to know. Each hue appears in a few saturated places only, against warm paper. Colour is never decoration. It is the label, so words can step back. The palette is calibrated with master-level restraint, each tint tested against its neighbour until it reads instantly at arm's length and from across a room.

Rhythm comes from repetition that rewards a second look. Rings ripple outward from each stone in dotted orbits, counted and evenly spaced. Tiny tick marks walk along the thread like footsteps. Small numerals sit beside each stone as quietly as plate numbers in a natural-history folio. The density of these marks is the work of patient, almost devotional labour: hundreds of identical gestures, each placed exactly, which together turn a simple path into an object of study, as if the act of asking a question could be measured.

Text is a whisper, never a voice. A stone may carry one word, set thin and widely spaced, and the sheet may carry one short line of italic serif, like a curator's caption. There are no paragraphs, no instructions and no arrows shouting. Typography sits inside the visual architecture with generous margins, never touching a form, never crowding an edge. The finished sheet should look as if it took a master craftsperson weeks: meticulously crafted, quietly exact, and simple enough that a ten-year-old understands it before reading a single letter.
